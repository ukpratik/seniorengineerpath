## What broke

This story is made up, but it happens in many companies. Priya is a mid-level backend engineer at a product company in Bengaluru. She closes more tickets than anyone on her team. Her code reviews are fast and careful.

In March she expects to become a senior engineer. Her manager tells her the promotion was not approved.

Priya is shocked. "I delivered more than anyone," she says. Her manager opens the company's career ladder. Priya has never read it.

The senior level asks for three things she has not done. Own a project with an unclear goal. Lead work with other teams. Help newer engineers grow. Priya did excellent mid-level work, faster than anyone. But she never showed the next level.

Her manager also made a mistake. He never showed her the ladder, and he never gave her work that could show those skills. Neither of them had read it closely until the day of the "no".

## The idea in simple words

A **career ladder** is a document that says what each job level expects. It has three parts: the levels, the dimensions, and the expectations for each level in each dimension.

The prereq lesson, [what senior really means](/learn/what-senior-really-means), explained scope, ambiguity and ownership. This lesson is about the document itself: how to read it and use it.

**Levels.** Many ladders go from junior to mid-level, senior, staff and principal. Names differ a lot. Dropbox uses IC1 to IC7 for engineers, and M3 to M7 for managers. [@dbx] CircleCI's 2018 matrix went from E1, for an Associate Engineer, up to E6, for a Principal Engineer. [@circleci] Rent the Runway's public ladder used names like Engineer II and Senior Engineer I. [@rtr]

**At GitLab, the manager track is a separate path, not a promotion.** GitLab says staff-level and engineering manager roles "are equivalent in terms of base compensation and prestige." [@gitlab] So at GitLab, moving to management is a change of job, not a step up. Many companies work the same way. Check your own ladder.

**Dimensions.** Each ladder groups skills differently. In this lesson we use five common areas:

- **Technical skill:** design, code quality, debugging.
- **Delivery and impact:** shipping work that matters.
- **Scope and ambiguity:** how big and how unclear your problems are.
- **Influence and communication:** how far your ideas reach.
- **People:** mentoring and helping others grow.

Dropbox's five core responsibilities are Results, Direction, Talent, Culture and Craft. [@dbx-ic3] Rent the Runway's ladder had the columns Technical Skill, Get Stuff Done, Impact, and Communication & Leadership. [@rtr] Each company's ladder is its own. Always read yours, not another company's.

> [!key] **The key idea.** A ladder describes the next level. You get promoted by showing evidence for it, not by doing your current level faster.

## How it works

Scope grows as you go up: task, then feature, then project, then many teams, then the org. Dropbox's ladder shows this in its own words. IC2 works "on defined projects to achieve team-level goals". [@dbx-ic2] IC3 owns "projects in service of quarterly goals on the team". [@dbx-ic3] IC4 owns "semi-annual/annual goals for my team", and its reach is "beginning to extend outside my team". [@dbx-ic4] IC5, the staff level, delivers "multi-year, multi-team product or platform goals". [@dbx-ic5]

The weight of each dimension also changes. Rent the Runway's notes say technical skill and getting things done are "the primary drivers of promotions at lower levels". At higher levels, the weight "gradually shifts" to impact, and to communication and leadership. [@rtr] Priya's story fits this pattern.

Here are the steps in words:

1. **Get the ladder.** Ask your manager for the current version. Read your level and the next one.
2. **Map your evidence.** For each dimension, write real examples with links: design docs, pull requests, incident notes.
3. **Find the gap.** Look for empty rows. One thin row matters more than three strong ones.
4. **Agree on it.** Show the map to your manager in a one-on-one. Ask: "Do you see the same gap?"
5. **Get the work.** Ask for a project that can show the gap. Then do it and record the results.

Ladders are guides, not checklists. Dropbox says its framework "is not a promotion checklist". [@dbx] CircleCI calls its matrix "a guideline" and expects "smaller deviations". [@circleci] Rent the Runway notes that not everyone grows in every column "in lockstep". [@rtr]

### The promotion process

At many companies, your manager writes or sponsors a promotion case. Will Larson suggests you also write your own promotion packet, early, as a guide. His template for staff promotions asks about your projects and their impact, people you mentored, your glue work, and your known gaps. [@larson-packets] In the end, it goes to "an internal promotion committee or process". [@larson-packets]

Many companies also hold calibration meetings. Larson describes them as "rounds of reviewing performance designations and reviews". Promotions are "typically also considered" there. [@larson-calibration] In calibration, people from other teams compare cases, so your evidence must be clear to strangers.

### Doing the job before the title

GitLab separates coaching from promotion. Coaching shows what you need to do. The promotion process "documents what an engineer has already done". [@gitlab] So you usually show the next level before you get it.

This has real limits. You may do bigger work for months at your old pay. Some steps also need an open role. At GitLab, moving from senior to staff or manager needs "a position of need". [@gitlab] Larson found that about a third of the staff engineers he surveyed changed companies to get the title. Roughly half of the women he spoke with had to change companies to get the staff title. [@larson-title] So the system is not always fair. Agree on clear goals and a timeline with your manager.

## Trade-offs

| Way to use the ladder | Gets you promoted? | Cost | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ignore it and work hard | No. Speed at your level rarely shows the next level. | Low effort now, a surprise later. | You find the gap on the day of the "no". |
| Treat it as a checklist | Sometimes. | You chase lines, not impact. | Ticked boxes with no real result do not convince a committee. |
| Map evidence and agree on the gap | Yes. This is the clearest path. | Regular talks with your manager, and some writing. | A manager change can reset the agreement. Keep it written down. |
| Switch companies for a title | Sometimes. | A new team and new trust to build. | The title may not mean the same level at the new company. |

**Titles do not travel.** Level names do not move cleanly between companies. Rent the Runway's ladder uses names like "Senior Engineer I". [@rtr] Dropbox uses codes like "IC3 Software Engineer". [@dbx] So compare scope and pay, not the word on the title. On Levels.fyi you can search self-reported pay by company, job title and career level. [@levels-fyi] For pay talks, see [negotiation and compensation](/learn/negotiation-and-compensation).

**Glue work.** Tanya Reilly uses "glue work" for things like unblocking teammates, reviewing design docs and onboarding new people. [@reilly-glue] She calls it technical leadership. But she warns that "doing glue work too early can be career limiting". [@reilly-glue] In her story, the engineer did so much glue work that she did not get better at coding. [@reilly-glue] If you do glue work, make it visible and connect it to a ladder dimension.

## At work: how a senior uses this

- Read the next level of your ladder this week. Write one line of evidence per dimension.
- Keep a "brag document": a running list of your work, with links and results. Julia Evans suggests this, and says many people update it "every 2 weeks". [@evans-brag]
- In your next one-on-one, ask: **"Which dimension is my weakest for the next level?"**
- Ask for work that tests that dimension. For influence, offer to write a [design doc](/learn/design-docs-and-rfcs) for a cross-team change.
- For the people dimension, mentor one newer engineer. See [mentoring and sponsorship](/learn/mentoring-and-sponsorship).
- Show impact in business terms, not only in tickets. [Working with PMs](/learn/working-with-pms-and-the-business) helps here.
- When you lead others, help them read their ladders too. Count their glue work in their reviews.
- When you change jobs, compare scope and pay, not titles. Interviewers check level too: see [big-tech interview loops](/learn/big-tech-interview-loops) and [behavioural interviews](/learn/behavioural-interviews-star).

## With AI

An AI assistant is useful for organising your evidence. It cannot judge your level. Only your company's ladder and the people in calibration can do that.

### What to ask the agent

> "Here is my company's ladder for the next level, and my list of work from the last six months. Map each piece of work to one dimension. List the dimensions with no evidence."

> "Rewrite these three project notes as short evidence lines. Each line should say what I did, what changed, and a number if I gave one. Do not add facts."

> "Here is the gap my manager and I agreed on. Suggest three kinds of work that would show it."

### What to check yourself

- Every line of evidence is true, and you can link to proof.
- The agent did not add numbers or results you never gave it.
- It used your company's ladder words, not general career advice.
- Your manager agrees with the gap. The agent's view does not count in calibration.
- You did not paste private company data into a tool your company does not allow.
